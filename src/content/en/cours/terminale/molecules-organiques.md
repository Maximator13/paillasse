> **In brief.** An organic molecule is a **carbon skeleton** (straight-chain, branched, unsaturated or cyclic) that carries **functional groups**. The functional group sets the **functional family** and the ending of the name (a prefix for a halogen); the skeleton sets the stem. Two molecules with the same molecular formula but different arrangements of atoms are **structural isomers**. A **polymer** repeats a unit derived from a monomer $n$ times.

## 1. Writing an organic molecule

The same species can be written in several ways, from the most complete to the most compact:

| Representation | What it shows | Example (propan-1-ol) |
|---|---|---|
| Molecular formula | type and number of atoms | $\mathrm{C_3H_8O}$ |
| Displayed formula | all the bonds | (all the $\mathrm{C{-}H}$ bonds drawn) |
| Structural formula | all the bonds except those to H | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}OH}$ |
| Skeletal formula | skeleton as a zigzag line | zigzag line with 3 carbons (2 segments) extended by OH |

> **Definition.** In a **skeletal formula**, the carbon chain is a zigzag line: each end and each vertex represents a carbon atom. The hydrogen atoms bonded to the carbons are not written; each carbon carries as many as it needs to be **tetravalent**. The other atoms (O, N, Cl…) and the H atoms bonded to them are written explicitly.

To go from the skeletal formula to the molecular formula, count the vertices (carbons), complete each carbon to four bonds with H atoms, then add the heteroatoms. A double bond is drawn as two parallel lines, a triple bond as three.

The carbon skeleton can be:

- **saturated**: only single $\mathrm{C{-}C}$ bonds (alkanes, general formula $\mathrm{C_nH_{2n+2}}$);
- **unsaturated**: at least one double bond $\mathrm{C{=}C}$ (alkenes, $\mathrm{C_nH_{2n}}$ for a single double bond with no ring) or triple bond;
- **branched**: a carbon bonded to at least three other carbons, as in $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH(CH_3){-}CH_3}$;
- **cyclic**: the chain closes on itself (cyclohexane $\mathrm{C_6H_{12}}$, a hexagon in skeletal form).

## 2. Functional groups and functional families

> **Definition.** A **functional group** is a group of atoms, containing at least one atom other than C and H, that gives the molecule particular chemical properties. Molecules carrying the same group form a **functional family**.

To the families seen in Première (alcohols, aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids), Terminale adds esters, amines, amides and halogenoalkanes.

| Family | Functional group | Example | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | hydroxyl $\mathrm{{-}OH}$ on a tetrahedral C | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}OH}$ | ethanol |
| Aldehyde | carbonyl at the end of the chain $\mathrm{{-}CHO}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CHO}$ | ethanal |
| Ketone | carbonyl within the chain $\mathrm{{-}CO{-}}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CO{-}CH_3}$ | propanone |
| Carboxylic acid | carboxyl $\mathrm{{-}COOH}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}COOH}$ | ethanoic acid |
| Ester | ester $\mathrm{{-}COO{-}}$ between two chains | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}COO{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$ | ethyl ethanoate |
| Amine | amino $\mathrm{{-}N{<}}$ (N bonded to C or H atoms) | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}NH_2}$ | ethanamine |
| Amide | amide $\mathrm{{-}CO{-}N{<}}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CO{-}NH_2}$ | ethanamide |
| Halogenoalkane | halogen X (F, Cl, Br, I) on a tetrahedral C | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CHCl{-}CH_3}$ | 2-chloropropane |

In an amide, the nitrogen atom is bonded **directly** to the carbon of the carbonyl group: this is what distinguishes it from an amine that also carries a ketone group.

## 3. Naming a molecule

The systematic name is built in three steps:

1. identify the **main chain**: the longest carbon chain containing the functional group (and the double bond if there is one); it gives the stem (meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-…);
2. **number** the chain so that the functional group (then the branches) gets the lowest number;
3. add in front of the stem the **branches** (methyl, ethyl…) and the halogen prefixes (chloro, bromo…), each preceded by its number, and finish with the **suffix** of the family.

| Family | Naming rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Alkene | stem + "-ene", position of the double bond | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH{=}CH{-}CH_3}$: but-2-ene |
| Ester | "alkyl alkanoate" | $\mathrm{H{-}COO{-}CH_3}$: methyl methanoate |
| Amine | stem + "-amine", substituents on N written "N-" | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}NH{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$: N-methylethanamine |
| Amide | stem + "-amide" | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CO{-}NH{-}CH_3}$: N-methylethanamide |
| Halogenoalkane | prefix "halogeno" + alkane | $\mathrm{CH_2Cl{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$: 1-chloropropane |

For an ester, the "alkanoate" part comes from the carboxylic acid (it contains the carbon of the $\mathrm{{-}COO{-}}$ group) and the "alkyl" part comes from the alcohol. So $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}COO{-}CH_3}$ is methyl propanoate: the chain on the C=O side has three carbons, the one on the O side has one.

## 4. Structural isomerism

> **Definition.** Two **structural isomers** have the same molecular formula but different structural formulae: the atoms are not connected in the same order. They have different physical properties (melting and boiling points, solubility) and often different chemical properties.

There are three cases:

- **chain isomerism**: the skeleton changes, the functional group stays the same. Butane $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$ and methylpropane $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH(CH_3){-}CH_3}$, both $\mathrm{C_4H_{10}}$;
- **position isomerism**: the functional group or the double bond changes place. Propan-1-ol $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}OH}$ and propan-2-ol $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH(OH){-}CH_3}$;
- **functional group isomerism**: the family changes. Propanoic acid $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}COOH}$ and methyl ethanoate $\mathrm{CH_3{-}COO{-}CH_3}$, both $\mathrm{C_3H_6O_2}$.

To check that two representations show isomers and not the same molecule, compare their **names**: two different names and the same molecular formula mean they are isomers.

## 5. Polymers

> **Definition.** A **polymer** is a macromolecule formed by repeating the same **repeat unit** a large number of times. It is obtained by a **polymerisation** reaction from small molecules, the **monomers**. The number $n$ of repeat units is the **degree of polymerisation**.

For an addition polymerisation, the repeat unit is obtained by "opening" the double bond of the monomer:

$$
n\ \mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2} \longrightarrow \mathrm{{-}\left(CH_2{-}CH_2\right)_{\mathit{n}}{-}}
$$

| Monomer | Repeat unit | Polymer | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| ethene $\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2}$ | $\mathrm{{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}}$ | polyethylene (PE) | bags, bottles |
| chloroethene $\mathrm{CH_2{=}CHCl}$ | $\mathrm{{-}CH_2{-}CHCl{-}}$ | polyvinyl chloride (PVC) | pipes |
| propene $\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH{-}CH_3}$ | $\mathrm{{-}CH_2{-}CH(CH_3){-}}$ | polypropylene (PP) | containers, textiles |

Polyesters (such as the PET used for bottles) and polyamides (such as nylon), on the other hand, contain ester or amide groups in their chain. The chains in a given sample do not all have the same length: we work with an average molar mass,

$$
M_{\text{polymer}} = n \times M_{\text{unit}}
$$

There are also many natural polymers: cellulose, starch, proteins (polyamides), DNA.

## Worked example

A glue contains ethyl ethanoate, with structural formula $\mathrm{CH_3{-}COO{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$. Data: $M(\mathrm{H}) = 1.0\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$, $M(\mathrm{C}) = 12.0\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$, $M(\mathrm{O}) = 16.0\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$, $M(\mathrm{Cl}) = 35.5\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$.

1. Give its molecular formula and its molar mass.
2. Write the structural formulae of its structural isomers that belong to the ester family, and name them.
3. Give one functional group isomer.
4. A sample of PVC has an average molar mass of $1.25 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$. Calculate its average degree of polymerisation.

1. There are 4 C, 8 H and 2 O: $\mathrm{C_4H_8O_2}$.

$$
M = 4 \times 12.0 + 8 \times 1.0 + 2 \times 16.0 = 88.0\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}
$$

2. The esters of formula $\mathrm{C_4H_8O_2}$ share the four carbons between the acid part and the alcohol part:
   - $\mathrm{H{-}COO{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}CH_3}$: propyl methanoate;
   - $\mathrm{H{-}COO{-}CH(CH_3){-}CH_3}$: 1-methylethyl methanoate (or isopropyl methanoate);
   - $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}COO{-}CH_3}$: methyl propanoate.

   With ethyl ethanoate, that makes four isomeric esters.

3. Butanoic acid $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}CH_2{-}COOH}$ has the same molecular formula $\mathrm{C_4H_8O_2}$: it is a functional group isomer.

4. The repeat unit of PVC is $\mathrm{{-}CH_2{-}CHCl{-}}$, of formula $\mathrm{C_2H_3Cl}$:

$$
M_{\text{unit}} = 2 \times 12.0 + 3 \times 1.0 + 35.5 = 62.5\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}
$$

$$
n = \frac{M_{\text{polymer}}}{M_{\text{unit}}} = \frac{1.25 \times 10^{5}}{62.5} = 2.00 \times 10^{3}
$$

## Key points

- In a skeletal formula, each vertex or end is a carbon, completed with H atoms up to four bonds.
- Ester $\mathrm{{-}COO{-}}$, amine $\mathrm{{-}N{<}}$, amide $\mathrm{{-}CO{-}N{<}}$, halogenoalkane $\mathrm{{-}X}$: each group gives its family and its ending (prefix "halogeno" for a halogenoalkane).
- An ester is named "alkyl alkanoate"; the main chain is numbered so that the functional group gets the lowest number.
- Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different arrangements of atoms (chain, position or functional group).
- Polymer: $n$ repeat units derived from monomers, with $M_{\text{polymer}} = n \times M_{\text{unit}}$.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting the **implicit H atoms** when going from the skeletal formula to the molecular formula, or counting a heteroatom as a carbon.
- Confusing **amine and amide**: in an amide, N is bonded to the carbon of the $\mathrm{C{=}O}$ group.
- Swapping the two parts of an ester's name: the "-oate" part contains the carbon of the $\mathrm{C{=}O}$.
- Taking two representations of the same molecule (chain drawn differently) for isomers: compare the arrangements of atoms, and therefore the names.
- Confusing **monomer** (starting molecule, with its double bond) and **repeat unit** (repeated unit, with no double bond for an addition polymerisation).
