> **The essentials.** An organic synthesis is designed as a sequence of steps: we choose the reactions that modify the chain or the functional groups, we speed up each step (temperature, catalyst) and we improve its yield (excess of one reactant, removal of a product). Where necessary, we protect a group that must not react, and we favour environmentally responsible processes.

## 1. The steps of a synthesis method

Every synthesis method has four main steps.

| Step | Aim | Common techniques |
|---|---|---|
| **Transformation** | get the reactants to react | heating under reflux, stirring, catalyst |
| **Separation** | isolate the crude product from the reaction mixture | solvent extraction (separating funnel), vacuum filtration, distillation |
| **Purification** | remove the remaining impurities | washing, drying (anhydrous magnesium sulfate), recrystallisation, fractional distillation |
| **Analysis** | identify the product and check its purity | melting point, thin-layer chromatography, IR spectroscopy, refractive index |

> **Definition.** **Heating under reflux** consists of heating the reaction mixture while condensing the vapours in a vertical condenser (bulb or straight): they fall back into the flask. This makes it possible to work at a high temperature **without any loss of material**.

The apparatus consists of a heating mantle, a round-bottomed flask containing a few pumice granules (to keep the boiling smooth) and a condenser in which the water enters at the bottom and leaves at the top. The temperature of the mixture is then fixed by the boiling of the solvent.

## 2. Optimising the rate of a step

The duration of a synthesis has a cost (energy, use of equipment). A transformation is sped up by acting on the **kinetic factors**:

- the **temperature**: thermal agitation increases the number of effective collisions between reacting species. This is the main benefit of heating under reflux;
- the **concentration** of the reactants: the higher it is, the more frequent the collisions;
- the **catalyst**: it speeds up the reaction without being consumed and without changing the final state. It opens up another, faster reaction pathway;
- the **choice of solvent**, which must dissolve the reactants to bring them into contact.

For esterification, concentrated sulfuric acid (or para-toluenesulfonic acid, PTSA) acts as the catalyst: it provides $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions which activate the carboxylic acid.

> **Note.** A catalyst speeds up the forward reaction and the reverse reaction alike: it allows the equilibrium state to be reached more quickly, but it **does not change the yield**.

## 3. Optimising the yield

> **Definition.** The **yield** $\eta$ of a synthesis is the ratio of the amount of product obtained to the maximum amount that could be obtained if the reaction went to completion.

$$
\eta = \frac{n_{\text{obtained}}}{n_{\max}}
$$

$n_{\max}$ is calculated from the **limiting reactant** and the stoichiometry. For a synthesis in several steps, the overall yield is the product of the yields of each step: $\eta = \eta_1 \times \eta_2 \times \dots$

A reaction that does not go to completion leads to a chemical equilibrium. The yield is then limited, for example to about 67% for the esterification of a primary alcohol with equimolar amounts:

$$
\mathrm{R{-}COOH + R'{-}OH \rightleftharpoons R{-}COO{-}R' + H_2O}
$$

The **equilibrium is shifted** in the forward direction in two ways:

1. **Adding one reactant in excess**, preferably the cheapest: the reaction quotient $Q_r$ decreases, becomes smaller than $K$, and the system evolves in the forward direction.
2. **Removing a product** as it forms, for example the water with a Dean-Stark apparatus or the ester by distillation: $Q_r$ stays smaller than $K$ and the transformation continues.

One reactant can also be replaced by a more reactive one (an acid anhydride instead of the carboxylic acid): the reaction then goes to completion. Finally, some of the product is lost during separation and purification: a careful method limits these losses.

## 4. Modifying a chain or a functional group

A synthesis modifies either the **carbon skeleton** (lengthening, shortening, branching, cyclisation, polymerisation) or the **functional groups**. Reactions are classified into three categories according to how the bonds change.

| Category | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Substitution** | an atom or group of atoms is replaced by another | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}Br + HO^- \rightarrow CH_3{-}CH_2{-}OH + Br^-}$ |
| **Addition** | two molecules join together; a multiple bond becomes a single bond | $\mathrm{CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O \rightarrow CH_3{-}CH_2{-}OH}$ |
| **Elimination** | a small molecule is removed; a multiple bond appears | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}CH_2{-}OH \rightarrow CH_2{=}CH_2 + H_2O}$ |

The category is recognised by comparing reactants and products: an addition makes a double bond **disappear**, an elimination **creates** one, a substitution keeps the structure while exchanging a group. Esterification is a substitution reaction: the $\mathrm{{-}OH}$ group of the acid is replaced by the $\mathrm{{-}O{-}R'}$ group.

## 5. Protection and deprotection

A polyfunctional molecule has several functional groups. A reagent may then attack a group that we want to keep: the reaction is not **selective**.

> **Definition.** **Protecting** a functional group means temporarily converting it into a group that is inert towards the reagent used. After the desired reaction, it is **deprotected** to recover the original group.

The strategy follows three steps: protection, transformation of the target group, deprotection. A good protection must be easy to put in place, withstand the conditions of the next step and be easily removed with a good yield.

A classic example: in peptide synthesis, to form the amide bond between two chosen amino acids, the amine group of one and the carboxyl group of the other are protected, otherwise a mixture of dipeptides would be obtained.

The price to pay: two extra steps, and therefore a lower overall yield, more reagents and more waste.

## 6. Towards an environmentally responsible synthesis

**Green chemistry** seeks to reduce the impact of a synthesis on the environment and on health. Its principles guide the choice of strategy:

- limit the **number of steps** (and therefore unnecessary protections);
- use **catalysts** rather than reagents in stoichiometric amounts;
- choose **solvents** of low toxicity (water, ethanol) or work without a solvent;
- save energy (room temperature, microwave heating);
- prefer reactants from **renewable resources**;
- maximise the **atom economy**: most of the atoms of the reactants should end up in the product. An addition is ideal from this point of view; a substitution or an elimination gives by-products.

## Worked example

3-Methylbutyl ethanoate (banana flavouring) is synthesised by heating under reflux a mixture of $V_1 = 20.0\ \mathrm{mL}$ of 3-methylbutan-1-ol, $V_2 = 30.0\ \mathrm{mL}$ of ethanoic acid and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid. After separation and purification, $m = 16.8\ \mathrm{g}$ of ester is obtained.

Data: 3-methylbutan-1-ol: $\rho_1 = 0.81\ \mathrm{g \cdot mL^{-1}}$, $M_1 = 88.1\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$; ethanoic acid: $\rho_2 = 1.05\ \mathrm{g \cdot mL^{-1}}$, $M_2 = 60.1\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$; ester: $M = 130.2\ \mathrm{g \cdot mol^{-1}}$.

1. What is the role of the sulfuric acid? Why heat under reflux?
2. Determine the limiting reactant.
3. Calculate the yield of the synthesis.

<details>
<summary>Show the solution</summary>

1. The sulfuric acid is a **catalyst**: it speeds up the reaction without changing the final state. Heating under reflux speeds up the transformation (high temperature) without any loss of reactants or products.

2. The initial amounts of substance are:

$$
n_1 = \frac{\rho_1 V_1}{M_1} = \frac{0.81 \times 20.0}{88.1} = 0.184\ \mathrm{mol}
\qquad
n_2 = \frac{\rho_2 V_2}{M_2} = \frac{1.05 \times 30.0}{60.1} = 0.524\ \mathrm{mol}
$$

The stoichiometric coefficients are equal to 1: the alcohol is the **limiting reactant**, the acid is in excess to shift the equilibrium.

3. The maximum amount of ester is $n_{\max} = n_1 = 0.184\ \mathrm{mol}$. The amount obtained is:

$$
n_{\text{obtained}} = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{16.8}{130.2} = 0.129\ \mathrm{mol}
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\eta = \frac{0.129}{0.184} = 0.70 \text{ i.e. } 70\%
$$

</details>

## Key points

- Method: **transformation, separation, purification, analysis**.
- **Heating under reflux** speeds up the reaction without any loss of material.
- The rate increases with the **temperature**, the **concentration** and a **catalyst**; the catalyst does not change the yield.
- The yield $\eta = \dfrac{n_{\text{obtained}}}{n_{\max}}$ increases with a **reactant in excess** or the **removal of a product**.
- Three categories of reactions: **substitution, addition, elimination**.
- A **protection** makes a synthesis selective, at the cost of two extra steps.

## Common mistakes

- Believing that a catalyst increases the yield: it only allows equilibrium to be reached more quickly.
- Calculating $n_{\max}$ with the reactant in excess instead of the **limiting** reactant.
- Forgetting the density: a volume of pure liquid is converted into a mass using $m = \rho V$, not $m = C_m V$.
- Confusing **addition** (a double bond disappears) with **elimination** (a double bond appears).
