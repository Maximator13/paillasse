> **The essentials.** The more readily an acid gives up its proton to water, the stronger it is. This ability is measured by the **acid dissociation constant** $K_A$ (or by $\mathrm{p}K_A$). A strong acid reacts completely with water ($\tau = 1$), a weak acid only partially ($\tau < 1$). Comparing the pH with the $\mathrm{p}K_A$ shows which form of the pair predominates: this is the key to acid-base indicators, amino acids and buffer solutions.

## 1. Acid dissociation constant and ionic product of water

An acid $\mathrm{HA}$ reacts with water according to the equilibrium:

$$
\mathrm{HA\,(aq) + H_2O\,(l) \rightleftarrows A^-\,(aq) + H_3O^+\,(aq)}
$$

> **Definition.** The acid dissociation constant $K_A$ of the pair $\mathrm{HA/A^-}$ is the equilibrium constant of this reaction:
>
> $$
> K_A = \frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]_{\text{eq}}\,[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]_{\text{eq}}}{[\mathrm{HA}]_{\text{eq}}\,c^\circ} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_A = -\log K_A
> $$
>
> with $c^\circ = 1\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$. $K_A$ depends only on temperature.

The larger $K_A$ is (and therefore the smaller $\mathrm{p}K_A$ is), the more the acid is dissociated: **the acid is stronger**. Its conjugate base is then weaker.

Water is an amphoteric species: it undergoes **autoprotolysis** $\mathrm{2\,H_2O\,(l) \rightleftarrows H_3O^+\,(aq) + HO^-\,(aq)}$.

> **Ionic product of water.** In any aqueous solution:
>
> $$
> K_e = \frac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]\,[\mathrm{HO^-}]}{c^{\circ 2}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-14} \quad \text{at } 25\ \mathrm{^\circ C}, \qquad \mathrm{p}K_e = 14.0
> $$

A solution is neutral if $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = [\mathrm{HO^-}]$, that is $\mathrm{pH} = 7.0$ at $25\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$. It is acidic if $\mathrm{pH} < 7.0$ and basic if $\mathrm{pH} > 7.0$.

The acid-base pairs of water set the limits of the scale: $\mathrm{H_3O^+/H_2O}$ has a $\mathrm{p}K_A$ of $0$ and $\mathrm{H_2O/HO^-}$ a $\mathrm{p}K_A$ of $14$.

## 2. Strong or weak acids and bases

> **Definition.** An acid (or a base) is **strong** in water if its reaction with water is **complete**. It is **weak** if the reaction is limited (equilibrium).

A weak base $\mathrm{A^-}$ reacts with water according to $\mathrm{A^-\,(aq) + H_2O\,(l) \rightleftarrows HA\,(aq) + HO^-\,(aq)}$.

For a given nominal concentration $C$, the final extent of reaction $x_f$ is compared with the maximum extent of reaction $x_{\max}$ using the **final degree of reaction**:

$$
\tau = \frac{x_f}{x_{\max}} \qquad \text{for an acid: } \tau = \frac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]_f}{C} = \frac{c^\circ \times 10^{-\mathrm{pH}}}{C}
$$

| | Strong acid | Weak acid | Strong base | Weak base |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reaction with water | complete, $\tau = 1$ | limited, $\tau < 1$ | complete, $\tau = 1$ | limited, $\tau < 1$ |
| Example | $\mathrm{HCl}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$ | $\mathrm{HO^-}$ (sodium hydroxide solution) | $\mathrm{NH_3}$ |
| pH (concentration $C$) | $\mathrm{pH} = -\log \dfrac{C}{c^\circ}$ | $\mathrm{pH} > -\log \dfrac{C}{c^\circ}$ | $\mathrm{pH} = 14 + \log \dfrac{C}{c^\circ}$ | $\mathrm{pH} < 14 + \log \dfrac{C}{c^\circ}$ |

For a weak acid, $\tau$ increases when the solution is diluted: dilution favours dissociation. To compare the strength of two acids, measure the pH of solutions of the **same concentration**: the stronger acid gives the lower pH.

## 3. Predominance and distribution diagrams

Taking the logarithm of the expression for $K_A$ gives a relationship between pH and $\mathrm{p}K_A$:

$$
\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_A + \log \frac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}
$$

It follows that:

- if $\mathrm{pH} < \mathrm{p}K_A$, then $[\mathrm{HA}] > [\mathrm{A^-}]$: the **acid form predominates**;
- if $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_A$, the two forms are present at equal concentrations;
- if $\mathrm{pH} > \mathrm{p}K_A$, the **basic form predominates**.

The **predominance diagram** summarises these cases on an axis graduated in pH, divided at the value of the $\mathrm{p}K_A$: $\mathrm{HA}$ on the left, $\mathrm{A^-}$ on the right.

The **distribution diagram** is more precise: it shows the percentage of each form as a function of pH. For the acid form:

$$
\%\,\mathrm{HA} = \frac{100}{1 + 10^{\,\mathrm{pH} - \mathrm{p}K_A}}
$$

The two curves cross at 50% for $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_A$: this is a graphical method for **reading off a $\mathrm{p}K_A$**. One form is in a large majority (more than 90%) as soon as the pH is one unit away from the $\mathrm{p}K_A$.

## 4. Acid-base indicators and alpha-amino acids

> **Definition.** An acid-base indicator is a pair $\mathrm{HIn/In^-}$ whose two forms have **different colours**.

As a first approximation, the colour of the acid form is observed for $\mathrm{pH} < \mathrm{p}K_A - 1$, and that of the basic form for $\mathrm{pH} > \mathrm{p}K_A + 1$. Between the two lies the **transition range**, where the colour is a mixture.

| Indicator | Acid form | Transition range | Basic form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methyl orange | red | $3.1$ to $4.4$ | yellow |
| Bromothymol blue | yellow | $6.0$ to $7.6$ | blue |
| Phenolphthalein | colourless | $8.2$ to $10.0$ | pink |

An **$\alpha$-amino acid** carries a carboxyl group $-\mathrm{COOH}$ and an amino group $-\mathrm{NH_2}$ on the same carbon atom. It therefore has two acid-base pairs. For glycine:

- $\mathrm{^+H_3N{-}CH_2{-}COOH / {}^+H_3N{-}CH_2{-}COO^-}$, with $\mathrm{p}K_{A1} = 2.3$;
- $\mathrm{^+H_3N{-}CH_2{-}COO^- / H_2N{-}CH_2{-}COO^-}$, with $\mathrm{p}K_{A2} = 9.6$.

The predominance diagram has three regions: the cationic form for $\mathrm{pH} < 2.3$, the **zwitterion** (or dipolar ion) $\mathrm{^+H_3N{-}CH_2{-}COO^-}$ between $2.3$ and $9.6$, and the anionic form beyond that. At physiological pH, the zwitterion predominates.

## 5. Buffer solutions

> **Definition.** A buffer solution is a solution whose pH changes **very little** when a moderate amount of acid or base is added, or on dilution.

It is prepared by mixing a weak acid and its conjugate base in similar amounts: the pH is then approximately equal to $\mathrm{p}K_A$. Added $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$ ions are consumed by $\mathrm{A^-}$, added $\mathrm{HO^-}$ ions by $\mathrm{HA}$: the ratio $[\mathrm{A^-}]/[\mathrm{HA}]$ changes little, and so does the pH.

Blood is buffered at $\mathrm{pH} = 7.4$, mainly by the pair $\mathrm{CO_2,H_2O/HCO_3^-}$ ($\mathrm{p}K_A = 6.1$). Buffer solutions are also used to calibrate pH meters ($\mathrm{pH} = 4.0$, $7.0$ and $10.0$).

## Worked example

A solution of ethanoic acid $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$ is prepared with a nominal concentration $C = 1.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$. Its measured pH is $3.4$.

1. Calculate the final degree of reaction. Is the acid strong or weak?
2. Deduce $K_A$ and $\mathrm{p}K_A$ for the pair $\mathrm{CH_3COOH/CH_3COO^-}$.
3. Which form predominates in the solution?

<details>
<summary>Show the solution</summary>

1. $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]_f = 10^{-3.4} = 4.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$, hence:

$$
\tau = \frac{4.0 \times 10^{-4}}{1.0 \times 10^{-2}} = 4.0 \times 10^{-2} = 4.0\%
$$

$\tau < 1$: the reaction with water is limited, so ethanoic acid is a **weak acid**.

2. From the equation, $[\mathrm{CH_3COO^-}]_f = [\mathrm{H_3O^+}]_f$ and $[\mathrm{CH_3COOH}]_f = C - [\mathrm{H_3O^+}]_f = 9.6 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$:

$$
K_A = \frac{(4.0 \times 10^{-4})^2}{9.6 \times 10^{-3}} = 1.7 \times 10^{-5} \qquad \mathrm{p}K_A = -\log(1.7 \times 10^{-5}) = 4.8
$$

3. $\mathrm{pH} = 3.4 < \mathrm{p}K_A = 4.8$: the acid form $\mathrm{CH_3COOH}$ predominates (it accounts for 96% of the acid introduced, which is confirmed by $\tau = 4.0\%$).

</details>

## Key points

- $K_A = \dfrac{[\mathrm{A^-}][\mathrm{H_3O^+}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]\,c^\circ}$: the smaller $\mathrm{p}K_A$ is, the stronger the acid.
- $K_e = \dfrac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}][\mathrm{HO^-}]}{c^{\circ 2}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ at $25\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$.
- **Strong** acid or base: $\tau = 1$; **weak**: $\tau < 1$.
- $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_A + \log \dfrac{[\mathrm{A^-}]}{[\mathrm{HA}]}$: acid form if $\mathrm{pH} < \mathrm{p}K_A$, basic form if $\mathrm{pH} > \mathrm{p}K_A$.
- An acid-base indicator changes colour around its $\mathrm{p}K_A$; a buffer solution has a pH close to the $\mathrm{p}K_A$ of the pair it contains.

## Common mistakes

- Believing that a weak acid is a dilute acid: strength depends on $K_A$, not on concentration.
- Applying $\mathrm{pH} = -\log C$ to a weak acid: this formula is valid only for a strong acid.
- Reversing the predominance diagram: the **acid** form is on the side of **low** pH values.
- Forgetting that $K_e$, $K_A$ and therefore neutrality ($\mathrm{pH} = 7$) depend on temperature.
