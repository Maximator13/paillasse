> **In brief.** An acid-base reaction is modelled by the **transfer of a hydrogen ion** $\mathrm{H^+}$ from an acid to a base. Each acid is associated with a conjugate base within a pair, and the acidity of an aqueous solution is measured by its **pH**, which is directly linked to the concentration of oxonium ions $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$.

## 1. Brønsted acids and bases

> **Definition.** A Brønsted **acid** is a chemical species able to **donate** a hydrogen ion $\mathrm{H^+}$. A Brønsted **base** is a chemical species able to **accept** a hydrogen ion $\mathrm{H^+}$.

When an acid, written $\mathrm{AH}$, donates a hydrogen ion, it turns into a species $\mathrm{A^-}$ which is in turn able to accept one: this is its **conjugate base**. The two species form an **acid-base pair**, always written in the order acid/base: $\mathrm{AH/A^-}$. The change from one to the other is summarised by a half-equation:

$$
\mathrm{AH = A^- + H^+}
$$

This notation is formal: the $\mathrm{H^+}$ ion does not exist in the free state in solution. An acid only donates a hydrogen ion if a base is present to accept it.

| Acid/base pair | Half-equation |
|---|---|
| $\mathrm{CH_3COOH/CH_3COO^-}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3COOH = CH_3COO^- + H^+}$ |
| $\mathrm{NH_4^+/NH_3}$ | $\mathrm{NH_4^+ = NH_3 + H^+}$ |
| $\mathrm{H_3O^+/H_2O}$ | $\mathrm{H_3O^+ = H_2O + H^+}$ |
| $\mathrm{H_2O/HO^-}$ | $\mathrm{H_2O = HO^- + H^+}$ |

The acid and the base of a pair differ only by one hydrogen ion: the charge of the base is therefore one unit lower than that of the acid.

## 2. Writing an acid-base reaction

> **Definition.** An acid-base reaction is a **transfer of a hydrogen ion** between the acid of one pair and the base of another pair.

With the pairs $\mathrm{A_1H/A_1^-}$ and $\mathrm{A_2H/A_2^-}$, it is written:

$$
\mathrm{A_1H + A_2^- \rightarrow A_1^- + A_2H}
$$

To establish the equation, proceed in three steps:

1. identify, among the species introduced, the acid of one pair and the base of the other;
2. write the half-equation of each pair in the direction in which it takes place (the acid donates, the base accepts);
3. add the two half-equations: the $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions must cancel out.

Example: a solution of ethanoic acid is mixed with a solution of ammonia.

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{CH_3COOH} &= \mathrm{CH_3COO^- + H^+} \\
\mathrm{NH_3 + H^+} &= \mathrm{NH_4^+} \\
\mathrm{CH_3COOH\,(aq) + NH_3\,(aq)} &\rightarrow \mathrm{CH_3COO^-\,(aq) + NH_4^+\,(aq)}
\end{aligned}
$$

Experimentally, a change in pH when two solutions are mixed, clearly greater than the effect of dilution alone, or a colour change of an indicator, shows that a transfer of a hydrogen ion has taken place.

## 3. The acid-base pairs of water and amphoteric species

Water belongs to two pairs:

- $\mathrm{H_3O^+/H_2O}$: water is the conjugate **base** of the oxonium ion;
- $\mathrm{H_2O/HO^-}$: water is the conjugate **acid** of the hydroxide ion.

> **Definition.** An **amphoteric** species can behave as an acid or as a base: it is the acid of one pair and the base of another.

Water is therefore amphoteric. It is by acting as a base that it reacts with hydrogen chloride to give hydrochloric acid:

$$
\mathrm{HCl\,(g) + H_2O\,(l) \rightarrow H_3O^+\,(aq) + Cl^-\,(aq)}
$$

**Carbonic acid** is carbon dioxide dissolved in water, written $\mathrm{(CO_2, H_2O)}$ or $\mathrm{H_2CO_3}$. It is involved in two successive pairs:

| Pair | Acid | Base |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{(CO_2, H_2O)/HCO_3^-}$ | carbonic acid | hydrogencarbonate ion |
| $\mathrm{HCO_3^-/CO_3^{2-}}$ | hydrogencarbonate ion | carbonate ion |

The hydrogencarbonate ion $\mathrm{HCO_3^-}$ is the base of the first pair and the acid of the second: it is amphoteric. To recognise an amphoteric species, look for a species that appears on the right in one pair and on the left in another.

## 4. Carboxylic acids, amines and Lewis structures

Two families of organic compounds have acid-base properties.

| Family | Acid of the pair | Base of the pair | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acids | carboxylic acid $\mathrm{R{-}COOH}$ | carboxylate ion $\mathrm{R{-}COO^-}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}COOH/CH_3{-}COO^-}$ |
| Amines | ammonium ion $\mathrm{R{-}NH_3^+}$ | amine $\mathrm{R{-}NH_2}$ | $\mathrm{CH_3{-}NH_3^+/CH_3{-}NH_2}$ |

Lewis structures explain these properties. A line between two atoms represents a **bonding pair** (a bond); a line placed on the edge of a single atom represents a **lone pair**.

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\begin{array}{ccccccc}
 & & \vert\mathrm{O}\vert & & & & \\
 & & \Vert & & & & \\
\mathrm{R} & - & \mathrm{C} & - & \overline{\underline{\mathrm{O}}} & - & \mathrm{H}
\end{array}
& \qquad &
\begin{array}{ccccc}
 & & \vert\mathrm{O}\vert & & \\
 & & \Vert & & \\
\mathrm{R} & - & \mathrm{C} & - & \overline{\underline{\mathrm{O}}}\vert^{\,\ominus}
\end{array}
\\[6pt]
\text{carboxylic acid} & & \text{carboxylate ion}
\end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
\begin{array}{ccccc}
\mathrm{R} & - & \overline{\mathrm{N}} & - & \mathrm{H} \\
 & & \vert & & \\
 & & \mathrm{H} & &
\end{array}
& \qquad &
\begin{array}{ccccc}
 & & \mathrm{H} & & \\
 & & \vert & & \\
\mathrm{R} & - & \mathrm{N}^{\oplus} & - & \mathrm{H} \\
 & & \vert & & \\
 & & \mathrm{H} & &
\end{array}
\\[6pt]
\text{amine} & & \text{ammonium ion}
\end{array}
$$

- In the **carboxyl group**, the $\mathrm{O{-}H}$ bond is polarised, because oxygen is much more electronegative than hydrogen: the hydrogen can leave as $\mathrm{H^+}$. The pair of electrons of the bond stays on the oxygen, which then carries three lone pairs and a negative charge.
- In an **amine**, the nitrogen atom carries a lone pair: it can use it to form a bond with an $\mathrm{H^+}$ ion. The nitrogen of the ammonium ion forms four bonds, no longer has a lone pair and carries a positive charge.

## 5. pH and its measurement

> **Definition.** The pH of an aqueous solution is related to the concentration of oxonium ions by:
>
> $$
> \mathrm{pH} = -\log\left(\frac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]}{c^\circ}\right)
> \qquad\text{that is}\qquad
> [\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = c^\circ \times 10^{-\mathrm{pH}}
> $$
>
> where $c^\circ = 1\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$ is the standard concentration. pH has no unit.

The base-10 logarithm function is increasing and the pH includes a minus sign: **the more concentrated the solution is in oxonium ions, the lower its pH**. Dividing $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]$ by 10 increases the pH by one unit. At $25\ \mathrm{°C}$, a solution is acidic if its pH is below 7, neutral if it equals 7, and basic above that.

This relationship is tested with hydrochloric acid $\mathrm{(H_3O^+, Cl^-)}$, for which $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]$ is equal to the concentration $c$ of the solution as prepared, by carrying out successive dilutions by a factor of 10:

| $c$ (in $\mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$) | $1.0 \times 10^{-2}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ | $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| expected pH | 2.00 | 3.00 | 4.00 |

The relationship is only valid for dilute solutions (concentrations below about $0.1\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$).

pH is measured with a **pH meter**: a probe immersed in the solution, connected to a unit calibrated beforehand with buffer solutions of known pH. The precision is of the order of 0.1 unit, which corresponds to an uncertainty of about 25% on $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]$. Writing rule: the number of **decimal places** of the pH is equal to the number of **significant figures** of $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]$ (a pH with one decimal place gives a concentration with one significant figure, and vice versa). pH paper only gives an estimate to the nearest unit.

## Worked example

A solution $S_0$ of hydrochloric acid $\mathrm{(H_3O^+, Cl^-)}$ of concentration $c_0 = 2.5 \times 10^{-2}\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$ is available.

1. Calculate the pH of $S_0$.
2. $S_0$ is diluted by a factor of ten. What is the pH of the solution $S_1$ obtained?
3. A solution of ammonia $\mathrm{NH_3}$ is added to $S_1$. Write the equation of the acid-base reaction that occurs.
4. The ammonia having been added in excess, the pH meter finally reads 9.2. Calculate the final concentration of oxonium ions.

**1.** In hydrochloric acid, $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = c_0$:

$$
\mathrm{pH} = -\log\left(2.5 \times 10^{-2}\right) = 1.60
$$

The concentration has two significant figures: the pH is written with two decimal places.

**2.** After dilution, $c_1 = \dfrac{c_0}{10} = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}$, so $\mathrm{pH} = -\log\left(2.5 \times 10^{-3}\right) = 2.60$. The pH has indeed increased by one unit.

**3.** The pairs involved are $\mathrm{H_3O^+/H_2O}$ and $\mathrm{NH_4^+/NH_3}$. The acid $\mathrm{H_3O^+}$ donates a hydrogen ion ($\mathrm{H_3O^+ = H_2O + H^+}$), the base $\mathrm{NH_3}$ accepts it ($\mathrm{NH_3 + H^+ = NH_4^+}$):

$$
\mathrm{H_3O^+\,(aq) + NH_3\,(aq) \rightarrow H_2O\,(l) + NH_4^+\,(aq)}
$$

**4.** The defining relationship is inverted:

$$
[\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = c^\circ \times 10^{-\mathrm{pH}} = 1 \times 10^{-9.2} \approx 6 \times 10^{-10}\ \mathrm{mol \cdot L^{-1}}
$$

The pH has only one decimal place: the concentration keeps only one significant figure (the calculator displays $6.3 \times 10^{-10}$).

## Key points

- An acid **donates** a hydrogen ion $\mathrm{H^+}$, a base **accepts** one. A pair is written acid/base.
- An acid-base reaction is a transfer of a hydrogen ion between two pairs: $\mathrm{A_1H + A_2^- \rightarrow A_1^- + A_2H}$.
- Water and the hydrogencarbonate ion are **amphoteric**.
- Pairs to know: $\mathrm{H_3O^+/H_2O}$, $\mathrm{H_2O/HO^-}$, $\mathrm{R{-}COOH/R{-}COO^-}$, $\mathrm{R{-}NH_3^+/R{-}NH_2}$, $\mathrm{(CO_2, H_2O)/HCO_3^-}$, $\mathrm{HCO_3^-/CO_3^{2-}}$.
- $\mathrm{pH} = -\log\left(\dfrac{[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]}{c^\circ}\right)$ and $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}] = c^\circ \times 10^{-\mathrm{pH}}$.

## Common mistakes

- Writing a pair the wrong way round: the acid always comes first.
- Leaving $\mathrm{H^+}$ ions in the final equation: they are exchanged, never free in solution.
- Confusing ammonia $\mathrm{NH_3}$ (base) with the ammonium ion $\mathrm{NH_4^+}$ (acid).
- Believing that pH increases with $[\mathrm{H_3O^+}]$: it is the opposite.
- Giving too many significant figures to a concentration calculated from a pH.
